# Darwinopterus modularis reference library

This folder is for anatomy review and generation QA. Use the sources to verify
specimen-backed structures and uncertainty; do not copy an existing artwork's
composition, surface treatment, or style.

## Diagnostic sources kept as links

- Lu et al., 2010, original description of `D. modularis`:
  https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2009.1603
  - Free-to-read version:
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2842655/
  - The `ZMNH M8782` holotype has a 185 mm skull and an estimated maximum
    wingspan of about 1074.9 mm.
  - The long, low skull has a confluent naris and antorbital fenestra,
    well-spaced spike-like teeth that are largest anteriorly, an elongated
    neck, and a long tail with more than 20 caudals stiffened by extended
    zygapophyses.
  - An aerial-predator interpretation was proposed but not demonstrated by a
    direct prey association or capture trace.
- Wang et al., 2010, restrictive Wukongopteridae comparison:
  https://doi.org/10.1590/S0001-37652010000400024
  - The paper cautions against treating every referred wukongopterid specimen
    as the same species and diagnoses `D. modularis` from its holotype.
  - Use the holotype's well-spaced spike-like teeth rather than transferring
    exact tooth counts or soft tissues from other taxa.
- Wei et al., 2021, Tiaojishan and Linglongta age synthesis:
  https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.11161
  - The Linglongta interval is summarized at about 161-160 Ma, within the
    Oxfordian or early Late Jurassic.
- Wang et al., 2006, Tiaojishan flora and palaeoclimate:
  https://doi.org/10.1080/10020070612330087A
  - The regional flora is dominated by Bennettitales, ferns, and
    Nilssoniales, followed by ginkgophytes, with less-common conifers and
    sphenopsids.
  - The regional climate is interpreted as warm and humid with a distinct
    seasonal signal.

## Evidence boundary

- Anchor species identity to the `ZMNH M8782` holotype. Referred
  wukongopterids do not automatically establish structures for
  `D. modularis`.
- The holotype supports a long, low skull, one confluent nasoantorbital
  opening, well-spaced spike-like teeth, an elongated neck, and one long stiff
  tail.
- The holotype preserves an incomplete longitudinal premaxillary crest.
  Cheng et al. 2017 provides a cautious reconstruction of its extent and
  possible soft-tissue continuation.
- Exact tooth count and fine tooth shape should not be over-fixed because
  specimen assignment, preservation, and later comparison affect those
  details. Use "well-spaced slender teeth, larger anteriorly."
- No species-level direct evidence for a terminal tail vane was found in the
  consulted `D. modularis` sources. Do not add a diamond, leaf, fork, or
  paddle vane.
- Fine body filaments, detailed membrane extent, coloration, feet, and skin
  surface are pterosaur-wide comparative reconstructions, not direct
  soft-tissue preservation in the holotype.

## Hard identity gates

- An Oxfordian wukongopterid pterosaur, not a dinosaur, lizard, crocodile,
  modern bird, bat, or dragon.
- One long, low, lightweight skull with one confluent nasoantorbital opening.
  Reject two separate rostral openings.
- A low longitudinal premaxillary crest extending over the skull roof toward
  the orbit, with a restrained serrated upper edge. Reject a missing crest,
  a tall casque, or a large posterior `Pteranodon`-like crest.
- Well-spaced slender teeth that become larger toward the front. Reject a
  toothless beak, uniform theropod teeth, or a dense crocodile-like row.
- Moderately elongated neck and a small compact torso.
- Exactly two forelimbs, and each forelimb is a wing. No separate birdlike
  arms beneath the wings.
- Each wing is supported by one continuous elongated fourth finger.
- Three small free clawed digits at each wrist when readable; these are not
  extra arms.
- Exactly two hind limbs and two separate feet in complete-body views.
- One continuous, long, stiff tail originating at the pelvis and tapering to
  a fine point.
- No terminal tail vane, second tail, split tail, or detached membrane.
- Reject extra heads, legs, wings, hands, tails, membranes, or duplicated
  joints.

## Habitat and behavior gates

- Use ash-dark volcaniclastic ground, tuffaceous ledges, narrow runoff
  channels, damp clearings, and a restrained palaeolake margin as regional
  context.
- Vegetation may include ginkgophytes, czekanowskiale foliage,
  Bennettitales, Nilssoniales, tree and ground ferns, horsetails, and
  irregular conifers.
- Avoid modern grass, flowers, palms, uniform pine plantations, and an
  exclusively open modern lake shore.
- Do not turn a fossil lake bottom or ash-deposition surface into the
  animal's only proven living surface.
- Flight, launch, ground stance, exact diet, capture method, and interaction
  with another animal remain reconstruction hypotheses.
- A distant feathered paravian may provide regional community context, but
  must remain separated and must not imply pursuit, predation, or direct
  association.

## Consistent phenotype

- Body filaments: smoky olive-brown.
- Wing membranes: mineral violet mixed with graphite.
- Accent: restrained ochre-copper on the low crest, cheek, and upper neck.
- Underside: pale reed and warm ivory.
- Pattern: broad asymmetric ash-and-lichen marbling with natural
  countershading. No regular stripes, dots, rings, checks, or neon.

## Six gallery roles

1. Full-body representative in a readable neutral glide.
2. Reverse-facing dawn color and membrane-pattern view of the same phenotype.
3. High-oblique post-rain volcaniclastic forest habitat view.
4. Intentional head-to-wrist crop emphasizing the low crest, one confluent
   opening, anteriorly larger teeth, three free wrist digits, and separate
   fourth wing finger; do not make distal limb or tail-count claims.
5. Fallen-ginkgo ground scene with a distant, non-contact feathered paravian
   as a regional community hypothesis.
6. Low-angle ventral ascent hypothesis showing two wings, two hind limbs, and
   one vane-free tail.
